Imagine yourself in the early days of the First World War, in a foreign country, barely a year old, and your father is already gone. Your mother can barely hear you. Money is a constant absence. The home is quiet in a way that has no explanation and offers none.

Most of us would struggle to inhabit that image fully — we can't easily cultivate the feeling of a world we haven't lived. But a man lived it exactly. His name was Albert Camus. Born in 1913 in French-occupied Algeria, he lost his father to the war before he could know him. His mother was partially deaf, illiterate, and spoke very little. The home he grew up in was not sad in a dramatic way — it was simply, persistently silent. A silence that asked nothing and explained nothing.

I came to know Camus through a YouTube video about The Myth of Sisyphus, and something in it pulled at me in a way I couldn't immediately put into words. The more I learned about him, the more I understood why. We share something in the texture of our early experience, even if the geography and the century are different.

I grew up in Damascus, in a neighborhood less than five hundred meters from the front line separating the city from Al-Ghouta. I was seventeen when the Arab Spring began, preparing for the high school diploma that felt like the only door out — the ticket from the slums toward something resembling a middle-class life. What followed was years of witnessed deaths, funerals across religious communities, and an anxiety that settled in my chest like a dead elephant that refused to move. It finally broke me at twenty-one, collapsing into a deep depression that was then compounded by the sudden death of my father from a stroke — leaving me, at twenty-two, as the sole breadwinner of my family.

I was suffering. For myself and for others. And I was questioning my existence without having the gift Camus had to describe it.

Fast forward to 2025. I'm living in Hamburg, travelling to Berlin to view apartments for a move. My girlfriend suggests a bookshop. And there it is, on the shelf, waiting: *The Myth of Sisyphus*, with the line printed on its cover — *"there is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide."* The baldness of it. The gravity. I knew immediately it was coming home with me.

---

Reading the book, I was stunned by how precisely Camus named something I had only ever described in street language — *all this hustle is for nothing*. He saw what most of us glimpse at some point: that life is enormously complex, but the complexity itself is meaningless. We suffer, sometimes enormously. We go through pain and agony. And none of it is answered. We scream at the universe for some solace, and the universe — cold, vast, impersonal — does not respond. It does not even register the question.

Camus calls this the absurd: not a property of the world alone, nor of humans alone, but the collision between the human need for clarity and the world's silence. And I agree with him completely. Our existence is absurd. We did not choose to be here. At some point we simply woke up on this rock floating in cold dark space, already mid-suffering, already mid-experience, with no say in the matter.

But then Camus asks: why do we even search for meaning in the first place? Why can't we simply accept the silence and move on? His answer is somewhat abstract — it is human nature, the condition of consciousness. I think the answer is more specific than that, and it lives in something he underestimates throughout the essay.

Language.

We are not simply meaning-seeking animals. We are language-speaking animals, and that distinction matters enormously. Language is not a neutral tool. It has grammar, causality, and an implied listener built into its very structure. Every sentence assumes someone on the other end. Because we experience existence through language, we inevitably attempt to understand existence through language as well — and that means we cannot encounter even an indifferent universe without, on some level, addressing it. The human cry for clarity that Camus describes is not irrational or delusional. It is the only way a language-using animal can meet a silent world. We cannot help but speak to it.

It is the same instinct that makes us talk to our pets. The same impulse behind the emotional connection people quickly form with AI chatbots the moment they begin to reply. The moment something responds, we lean in. We are wired for exchange. When the universe withholds it, we experience that absence as a specific kind of wound.

Camus identifies the absurd but does not examine this mechanism underneath it. Without accounting for the linguistic structure of human consciousness, the absurd remains a philosophical observation. I think it needs to become an anthropological one.

---

And language does not only make us meaning-seekers. It makes us storytellers.

We do not live in meanings — we live inside narratives. Self-narrated stories that give shape to who we are, where we came from, and crucially, where we might be going. And these stories never exist in isolation. They intermesh with the stories of the people around us — family, community, society — in a constant, living conversation. Our story is never entirely ours alone.

This is where I arrive at something Camus touches but does not fully develop: the question of what actually breaks a person.

The absurd does not consume those who discover that life is meaningless. It consumes those who lose the ability to imagine the next chapter. When a person's story no longer fits the world around them — when it feels terminally stuck, when the imagination cannot find a way forward — that is when alienation sets in. That is when the giving up happens. The crisis is not philosophical. It is narrative. It is a writer's block of the self.

I watched this happen to a friend of mine who took his own life. I watched it in my father — years of financial decisions that compounded into isolation, and that isolation into organ failure. And I lived it myself, in my final year at university, as an artificial intelligence graduate in a war-torn country without reliable electricity, unable to see any version of a future that had me in it.

The moment my self-narrative failed to turn the page, I began to fold under the weight of that dead elephant. It was not the absurd that broke me. The absurd was a symptom. The root cause was the story being blocked. No next chapter. No imaginable forward direction.

---

This is also, I think, visible in the myth of Sisyphus itself — more than Camus acknowledges.

Sisyphus was a man of language. Cunning, manipulative, persuasive enough to deceive the gods themselves. His crime was essentially a crime of communication — of bending meaning to his will. And his punishment was to be placed in a space where language is completely useless. The boulder does not listen. The mountain does not respond. There is no one to negotiate with, no story to tell, no next chapter to write.

Camus sees this and concludes: we must proceed anyway. Even when language cannot help us, even when no new chapters are possible, we push on in an act of defiant awareness. He tells us to imagine Sisyphus happy at the top of the mountain, watching the boulder roll down, choosing to descend and begin again.

I cannot follow him there. And not only as a philosophical disagreement — I know from my own experience that "proceed anyway" is not always available to a person. I could not simply move forward when I did not know whether a rocket would end my existence on some random afternoon. Agency matters. The story needs at least the possibility of authorship. What saved me was not defiance — it was finding a hand that could help me believe another chapter was possible. The way Dante needed his guide to walk with him through hell before he could find himself on the other side.

But while I was searching for that help, I found something else that kept me standing. Not meaning. Not defiance. Routine.

And here is where I arrive at my real disagreement with Camus — and it comes, fittingly, from the opposite direction of his own observation. He notices how the absurd sneaks up on the person *within* routine — the factory worker who, mid-commute one ordinary day, suddenly asks: what is the meaning of all this? The routine cracks open and the void shows through.

My journey ran in reverse. I was drowning in chaos, in unpredictability, in the constant threat of annihilation. What I needed was not to be shaken out of routine — I needed routine to hold me in place. To root me. To give the body something to do when the mind had lost the plot.

---

This is why I do not want to imagine Sisyphus happy at the top of the mountain.

I want to imagine him deliberately pushing the boulder back down himself — in full anticipation of the fulfillment that comes from pushing it up again. Not happiness. Not defiance. Fulfillment. The quiet satisfaction of the act itself, the rhythm of it, the way it roots him in a continuity that the gods intended as punishment but that became, over time, something closer to home.

The universe does not move in straight lines. It moves in spirals. Planets orbit. The sun rises and sets from the same directions every day. The body returns to the gym and lifts the same weights, and there is no final exercise that unlocks permanent health — only the continuity of the practice. This is not defeat. This is the actual shape of existence.

Camus says we must push through in stubborn defiance, knowing it is meaningless. I disagree. It is not defiance that sustains us — it is the capacity to find fulfillment in the act itself. The search for meaning implies a destination, a final answer, a last page. But if this universe moves in spirals, then orienting a life around fulfillment rather than meaning is not resignation. It is the more honest alignment with what existence actually is.

The story may not have a final chapter. But as long as the imagination can keep writing, the next one is always possible.
